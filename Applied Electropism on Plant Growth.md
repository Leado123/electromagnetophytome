**The Electromagnetophytome and Electromagnetophytomics (EMP and EMPics): The Effects of Electromagnetic Phenomena on Plant Growth**  
Leo Wen and Jason Wen, John F. Kennedy Middle College High School  
**Abstract**  
Fertilizer use in the agricultural industry is unsustainable and also a cause of climate change (Manthiram & Gribkoff, 2019), and the use of electromagnetic (EM) phenomena to stimulate plant growth, combined with renewable energy sources, could help reduce the use of nitrogen-based fertilizers. Many reductionist approaches to studying the EM effects of  phenomena (fields/currents) on plant growth often neglect to analyze genetic and morphological factors, leading to mixed results. The goal of this project is to study the many effects of electromagnetic phenomena on plant growth using a systematic, data-driven approach and to establish the electromagneto \-phytome (EMP), which relates genomic and morphological factors to a plant’s response to EM phenomena. We also aim to establish electromagnetophytomics (EMPics) to prove a genetic basis for EM response. To achieve this end, we will construct a novel, high-throughput autonomous phenotyping system using robotic “gantries” and computer vision algorithms. This system will profile the EMP for Arabidopsis thaliana and Glycine max seedlings and tissue cultures. A selective breeding experiment will also be performed on two profiled *A. thaliana* strains with opposing EMPs to test for genetic causes.  
**Introduction**  
	The effects of EMP on plants are well recorded and documented under controlled environments (Dannehl, 2018). Its potential as an agricultural practice (electroculture) captured the attention of many physicists in the 20th century; however, its use never became widespread due to mixed results when attempted in an agricultural setting. The effects have not been widely accepted by the scientific community due to the lack of a sufficient understanding explanation as to why EM phenomena affect the phenotypes of plants (plant growth), especially because electromagnetic phenomena are multivariate: AC (wave shape, field/current, amount of current, etc.), DC (current), alternating magnetic fields (AMF, similar variables to AC), constant magnetic fields (CMF, similar variables to DC), and not to forget exposure time. We aim to resolve these problems by collecting and recording data describing the effects of a large number of electromagnetic multivariables (EMM) on plant inductance.  
	Lemström (1908) wrote a book titled “Electricity in Agriculture and Horticulture (Electroculture)” introducing a novel method that improves plant growth underneath a charged Faraday's Cage. In 1918, the British Electroculture Committee was created by physicists to investigate the applications of Lemstrom’s electroculture as a method for improving agricultural productivity (Kinahan, 2009). Much of their preliminary work showed great potential for their method, but it was later revealed that they struggled to obtain consistent results when doing large-scale agricultural experiments. They also ignored the “why” during their research because they were focused only on maximizing their yields.   
In 2022,  Leo performed experiments investigating the effects of DC on plant growth and found that different voltages had different effects on pinto bean growth, and that even among the same species, different individual plants experienced extraordinary diversity in how they grew in response to DC; with half of the “electrified” plants growing faster than the control and the other half growing slower. Dziwulsak et al. (2023) also uncovered, using “"k-means clustering” (explain)", that soybeans’ growth response to EM tended to be affected more by their variety than by the type of electromagnetic phenomena that were applied to them. Both Leo’s & Dziwulsak et al.’s results show that genetics certainly plays a role in electromagnetic induction of plant growth. Dziwulsak et al. also found that different varieties of soybeans have a preference for either AC, AMF, or CMF, and that this preference remains even when the plant is under different developmental stages (seed sprouting, pot-culture).  
	Because many studies report that exposing plants (or seeds) to controlled electric and/or magnetic fields can alter measurable growth-related traits, it is reasonable to treat EM exposure as an experimental input and growth response as a quantitative output. Moreover, genotype often significantly affects these responses, implying that the same electromagnetic condition can produce different phenotypic outcomes in different cultivars or species.  
Therefore, for each individual (and more generally, for each genotype or species), an “electromagnetophytome” (EMP) can be defined as a response surface: a multivariable mapping from electromagnetic parameters (AC/DC/AMF/CMF, intensity, frequency, waveform features, and exposure duration) to growth values normalized to controls. The EMP will allow for a methodical and data-driven approach to making electroculture viable for agriculture, as well as in-vitro analysis. The EMP will also explain why electroculture sometimes works and sometimes doesn't, with standard deviation as an integral part of each datapoint. As a result of EMP, electromagnetophytomics (EMPics) exists as well. EMPics will help prove a genetic precedent for EMP phenomena by selecting distinct EMP individuals from a species and seeing how recombination and generating homozygous lines affect their offspring’s EMP. A genetic underpinning will also give precedence for attempting to change the genome of a species to work better under electroculture (directed evolution, selective breeding, GMO, etc.)  
Previous approaches focus only on one or two problems, but it is important to address all three for the basis of good scientific discovery. The Electroculture Committee focused only on addressing the issue of applied electroculture and ignored the “why.” Leo’s previous experiment only focused on DC and two voltages (4V and 6V). Dziwulsak et. al. had a broader focus on AC, AMF, and CMF, but they still did not fully assess the complete EMP. Profiling the EMP is a step towards the solution for all three problems, and accepts the multivariate nature of EM plant induction. Profiling the EMP will also take into account genetic differences; therefore, we will phenotype clones of a specific individual to reduce the standard deviations observed by afromentioned authors. Most importantly, sequencing the EMP makes clear that the “why” is the most important part of scientific investigation, of which EM induction has been deprived for over a century.  
The structure of the EMP relies heavily on species, physiological state, and the number of variables being studied. If varieties of soybean can exhibit tremendous EMP difference, then the difference between species seems unimaginable. Therefore, the EMP is probably best studied within the confines of species (preferably varieties) but not individuals, for which there is no practical application. Despite the EMP being best confined to species/varieties, for *A. thaliana*, we will phenotype a specific individual; that is, because it is important to see other causes of EMP variation that are not genetic, such as physiology. The physiological state of the plant being studied is also very important; Dziwulsak et al. (2023) have found that plant induction under the same EM conditions is at different scales between seedlings and adults of the same genome. Therefore, we will phenotype both seedling EMP (sprouting rates) and in vitro/tissue culture EMP (Phenomena phenotype analysis). The seedling EMP and in vitro EMP can potentially be mapped to each other through semi-supervised manifold alignment because their underlying cause is genetic. The number of variables is also important to study. For our experiment, our variables will be many: AC/DC/AMF/CMF, voltage, Teslas (AMF, CMF), wave shape, and exposure time/frequency.

**Plan**  
To obtain data on those variables quickly, an automated high-throughput phenotyper seems necessary. Therefore, we will make one similar to this rough 3D sketch below:

The high throughput “profiler” is composed of a 2 x 12 array (not fully filled because there needs to be “swap space” for moving vessels around) of polypropylene tissue culture containers with polyvinyl chloride (PVC) foil lids that have 2 graphite electrodes attached with silicone sealant. These containers are picked up and moved around by robots constrained to PVC pipe tracks power-sourced away from the PVC tracks to prevent tangling. There is also a hanging light above the setup. The last 4 columns in the matrix allow for the tissue culture containers to be exposed to a specific EM phenomenon for one hour (default setting) with custom multivariable control by the server and the Analog Discovery 3\.   
	Plant growth is measured every 1-4 hours and after each EM exposure, using a low-cost version of the setup used in Bethge et al. (2023). Robots pick up these containers to be observed by a camera \+ ToF (time of flight) setup, which uses a ringlight and multi-color LED arrangement to illuminate the image. The images are timestamped and analysed with a computer vision Python pipeline to assess plant growth and health.  
	The server controls the robots through WiFi and coordinates their behaviors, as well as programmatically generating the correct multivariable EM at the right time with the Analog Discovery 3\. Every day, the server establishes daily “goals” of doing certain electromagnetic combinations to try on plants; long-term experiment “goals” are to be set by the user at the beginning of the experiment.  
Every day, the server will perform local automated data analysis and determine which tests to do next using a computer algorithm. In short, it will redo tests automatically if the results are not statistically significant and generate novel tests based on whether the computer determines they will contribute meaningfully to the profiling of the EMP. The novel tests are also a way to introduce variety without wasting too much time.  
An *A. thaliana* individual will be cloned to do this sequencing to identify non-genomic contributors to diverse EMP. Multiple soybeans will be profiled as well. A “gene library” will be created to store all of these plants for later experiments.  
Another experiment to be done is an inheritance project. Several *A. thaliana* individuals from the gene library will be screened for distinct EMP phenotypes. The 2 individuals with the most opposite EMPs will then be further profiled for double-checking, and then cultivated in soil. Once the 2 strains flower, they will be selectively bred to find out the inheritance pattern of the EMP. GA3 may be used to enhance flowering rates. The resulting seeds will be profiled to analyse the results.  
The seed-sprouting (seedling) counterpart EMP of *A. thaliana* will also be profiled using the same setup. Once the seedling EMP is sequenced, a semi-supervised manifold alignment software can be used to assess the relationship between seedling EMP and in-vitro EMP (Ham, 2005).  
This proposal also analyzes the EMP’s important basic and applied uses. The EMP will help choose distinct EMP individuals for the breeding experiment. The EMP will also help in experiments implementing electroculture. EMPics will also assess clusters generated by UMAP to observe patterns underlying the data obtained, and support a genetic hypothesis through semi-supervised manifold alignment between in vitro and seedling EMP. The EMP can also have potential uses in triggering specific tissue growth in tissue culture; a focus will be placed on trying to increase the chance of flower induction in-vitro.  
**Implementation**  
For this project, the first thing is to make the gene library. A gene library is a collection of continually propagated clones. We have already started making a gene library for soybeans to practice making tissue culture. Another thing to do is to make the server, robots, and physical setup. The server will most likely be written in Typescript for its easy RPC communication features for robot control. The image analysis pipeline will be in Python. The server will be tested before deployment.   
After the server is made, the project can be implemented. First, we will profile *A. thaliana* to be able to get the bare minimum EMP. After this, the *A. thaliana* seedling EMP can be profiled with the same automated setup. The *A. thaliana* gene library will be screened for EMP distinct strains, which will then be used for a breeding experiment. The experiment will allow 4-6 weeks for the 2 strains’ multiple clones to flower and be selectively bred, before testing the EMP of their offspring. Soybeans will also be profiled due to their agricultural importance. Extra experiments can be attempted in the meantime to better understand the uses of the EMP, such as in the formation of specialized tissue, such as flowers. Real electroculture applications are also investigated to see if the EMP can be used to improve yields in a simulated agricultural environment (outdoors).  
Key milestones are the sequencing of *A. thaliana*’s in vitro EMP, *A. thaliana*’s seedling EMP, and the successful completion of the *A. thaliana* genetic experiment. Those aforementioned milestones will be completed by March, April, and May, respectively. The other accomplishments before are not as crucial as the ones mentioned before.  
There are many tests to evaluate the project. For example, if the *A. thaliana*'s profile has a low standard deviation on all of its points, then it can be said that the quality of the data is pretty high. However, if there is high deviation on some of the points, but there is heavy coverage (reexaminations) of the same point, then it can still be high quality and just shows that there are physiological factors affecting EM induction. Other tests are too long to explain in this proposal.  
	The proposed automated lab is not a small undertaking, so in order to complete the research by the deadline, we plan on dividing the work and responsibilities, with Leo handling most of the biological/analytical parts of the project, and Jason handling the engineering aspect of the automated lab. We plan on facilitating collaboration by meeting and working together daily (or at least 5 times a week if that isn’t possible) to discuss project progress and make necessary adjustments if needed.  
 	The most likely issue we will encounter with this project is that there will be physical challenges preventing the automation from working. While we presented one physical setup of the sequencing with robots, there are several other possible solutions; the one presented was simply the cheapest and most cost-effective. There are many planned contingencies in order from easiest to most menial: a rotational platform holding the in-vitro plants, and a linear actuator to move things around, etc.  
	Another issue that may occur is that the automated profiling will fail because of a software issue. These issues can be prevented by testing the software beforehand.  The most common issue throughout would be mold growing in the tissue cultures. The best way to prevent issues with mold is to practice proper sterile technique when preparing the plant containers and making the profiler allow unforeseen pauses.  
Even with all of these preventive measures, it is still likely that something fails for an unforeseeable reason the first time. However, the automated setup is designed to profile a plant species to the bare minimum in around 6 days, which is hopefully short enough so that no parts will break.
